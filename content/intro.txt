# reading | Why read the Gītā, and how
The *Bhagavad Gītā*, "the Song of the Blessed One," is seven hundred verses long, about the length of a short novella. It has probably been commented on, translated and argued over more than any other Indian text. It has been called the essence of the Vedas, a gospel of action, a manual of meditation, a hymn of devotion, a defense of caste, an allegory of the inner war, and a justification of violence. Each of these readings has a foothold in the text. That is one reason the Gītā rewards slow, serious study.
The best way to read it is as what it presents itself to be: a dialogue at a moment of crisis. A man who must act cannot. His teacher does not give him a rule but walks him, step by step, through a sequence of arguments. Each argument answers the last objection and opens a deeper question. The Gītā is less a treatise than a course of instruction. Its claims are pitched at different levels, and later teachings qualify earlier ones. A verse read out of its place in that sequence can mean almost the opposite of what it means within it.
This companion therefore keeps the order of the text but adds layers you can open as needed. These are footnotes on difficult verses, a **reconstruction** of each section's argument in its own terms, an **analysis** of its structure and tensions, its **sources**, its place in the **wider conversation** of philosophy and religion, and **questions** for discussion. A good first reading is simply the verses with the footnotes. A second reading is the study panels. A third is your own notes.

# text | The text: place, date and form
The Gītā is not a free-standing book. It forms Chapters 23–40 of the *Bhīṣma Parvan*, the sixth of the eighteen books of the [[Mahābhārata]]. It is spoken on the first morning of the great war, as the armies face each other at [[Kurukṣetra]]. The blind king [[Dhṛtarāṣṭra]] asks his charioteer [[Sañjaya]], who has been given divine sight, what happened. The whole dialogue between [[Kṛṣṇa]] and [[Arjuna]] is Sañjaya's report.
Dating is uncertain. Most scholars place the composition of the Gītā somewhere between the 2nd century BCE and the 1st century CE, with the epic itself growing over a longer period (c. 400 BCE–400 CE). Arguments for dating rely on its relation to the Upaniṣads (which it quotes), to early Buddhism (whose word *nirvāṇa* it adopts), to the classical systems of Sāṅkhya and Yoga (whose vocabulary it uses in pre-systematic form), and to the rise of devotional worship of Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa, attested in inscriptions from the 2nd century BCE (for instance the Heliodorus pillar at Besnagar, c. 110 BCE).
Was the Gītā always part of the epic, or inserted later? Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century German scholars (Richard Garbe, Rudolf Otto and others) tried to peel the text into an "original" core and later "interpolations," usually a theistic or Vedāntic layer added to an older heroic or Sāṅkhya poem. More recent scholarship (Angelika Malinar, James Fitzgerald, Alf Hiltebeitel, and the critique of German Indology by Vishwa Adluri and Joydeep Bagchee) has been sceptical of these reconstructions. It reads the Gītā as a deliberately composed whole, written with the epic in view. This companion follows that approach: the text is read as it stands.
The Gītā is written in the *anuṣṭubh* (*śloka*) meter, with longer *triṣṭubh* stanzas at moments of heightened intensity, above all in the vision of Chapter 11. Each chapter ends with a colophon calling the Gītā an *upaniṣad*, a "science of brahman" and a "scripture of yoga." The 700-verse text is the one Śaṅkara commented on in the 8th century. A slightly longer Kashmir recension, used by Abhinavagupta, differs in a few verses.

# epic | The epic setting: the war and its people
The Mahābhārata tells of a dynastic feud. Two brothers, the blind Dhṛtarāṣṭra and Pāṇḍu, have sons. Dhṛtarāṣṭra's hundred sons, the [[Kauravas]] led by [[Duryodhana]], refuse to share the kingdom with Pāṇḍu's five sons, the [[Pāṇḍavas]]. The Pāṇḍavas are cheated at dice, their shared wife Draupadī is humiliated in the assembly hall, and they are sent into thirteen years of exile. When they return and ask for their share, even five villages, Duryodhana refuses. War follows, and nearly every warrior in India is drawn in on one side or the other.
The war is a war among kin. On the Kaurava side stand [[Bhīṣma]], great-uncle to both parties, and [[Droṇa]], who taught them all to fight. With them is [[Karṇa]], who is secretly the Pāṇḍavas' eldest brother. Kṛṣṇa, cousin and friend to the Pāṇḍavas, has refused to bear arms but agreed to drive Arjuna's chariot. That situation is the Gītā's point of departure. The enemy is one's own family, the cause is just but the cost is monstrous, and the person beside you is God.
It matters that the Gītā comes at the *beginning* of the war. Everything that follows in the epic tests what it teaches: the deaths of Bhīṣma, Droṇa and Karṇa (each by a stratagem Kṛṣṇa advises), the near-extermination of both sides, and the joyless victory. The epic does not let the Gītā's answers go unchallenged. Readers who know only the Gītā miss this dialogue between the teaching and its aftermath.

# dilemma | Arjuna's dilemma: the problem of action
Arjuna's objections in Chapter 1 are not cowardice, though Kṛṣṇa first treats them so. They are a set of serious moral arguments. Killing kin brings evil {1.36}. It destroys the family's ritual and moral order {1.40}. That leads to social chaos and the ruin of the ancestors {1.41–42}. Winning a kingdom is worthless if those one wanted to share it with are dead {1.32–33}. Better to die unresisting {1.46}.
Philosophers have recognized here a genuine *moral dilemma*, a situation in which every option violates a serious obligation. Arjuna's duty as a warrior to fight a just war collides with his duty to family and teachers. Amartya Sen has argued that Arjuna's consequence-sensitive reasoning deserves more respect than the tradition has given it. Bimal Krishna Matilal used the episode to show that classical Indian thought took moral conflict seriously. In Arjuna's own words his mind is "confused about dharma" {2.7}.
Beneath the moral dilemma lies a metaphysical one. In the world the Gītā inherits, *all* action binds. Every deed produces fruits that must be reaped, in this life or another, keeping the agent on the wheel of rebirth. The obvious way out, taken by many renunciant movements of the time (Upaniṣadic, Buddhist, Jain), was to withdraw from action as far as possible. Arjuna's "I will not fight" {2.9} sounds like that renunciant's answer. Kṛṣṇa's task is to show that withdrawal is neither possible {3.5} nor the real meaning of renunciation. The real meaning is to act without being bound.

# worldview | The inherited worldview: karma, saṁsāra, mokṣa
Three linked ideas form the background against which the Gītā speaks. **[[karma|Karma]]** first meant ritual action, but by the Upaniṣads it named the moral causality by which actions ripen into consequences. **[[saṁsāra|Saṁsāra]]** is the round of birth and death those consequences sustain. **[[mokṣa|Mokṣa]]** (liberation, also called *nirvāṇa*) is release from that round.
In this frame, the deepest question is not "what is the right thing to do?" but "how can any doing be compatible with freedom?" The Gītā's distinctive answer is that the binding force lies not in action itself but in *attachment* (*saṅga*) and *desire* (*kāma*) for its fruit. Action done from equanimity, as sacrifice, or as an offering to God, leaves no residue. "Your right is to action alone, never to its fruits" {2.47} is the formula.
Alongside this soteriology runs the social order of **[[dharma]]**: the four classes ([[varṇa]]), the stages of life ([[āśrama]]), and the duties proper to each. The aims of life ([[puruṣārtha]]) are dharma, wealth, pleasure and liberation. Much of the Gītā's originality lies in holding together the world-affirming order of dharma and the world-transcending goal of liberation, which other traditions set at odds.

# sankhya | Sāṅkhya: nature, person and the three guṇas
**[[Sāṅkhya]]** ("enumeration") supplies the Gītā's psychology and much of its cosmology. Reality has two irreducible principles. **[[puruṣa|Puruṣa]]**, the "person," is pure consciousness: inactive, unchanging, a witness. **[[prakṛti|Prakṛti]]**, "nature," is everything else. It is the dynamic matrix that evolves into understanding (*buddhi*), I-making (*ahaṅkāra*), mind (*manas*), the senses, and the elements.
Nature is woven from three strands, the **[[guṇa|guṇas]]**. **Sattva** is clarity, lightness and goodness. **Rajas** is passion, activity and craving. **Tamas** is darkness, inertia and delusion. Every thing, act and state is a mixture of the three. Chapters 14, 17 and 18 use this scheme to classify knowledge, action, agents, faith, food, gifts and happiness.
The key Sāṅkhya insight for the Gītā is that *all action belongs to nature*. "Actions are performed in every case by the guṇas of nature, but one deluded by I-making thinks, 'I am the doer'" {3.27}. Bondage is the self's mistaken identification with nature's activity. Freedom begins with seeing that the self is not the agent. The Gītā's Sāṅkhya, however, is not yet the atheistic classical system of Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṅkhya-kārikā*. It is theistic. Nature is God's nature, and both principles are his "lower" and "higher" natures {7.4–5}.

# upanisads | Upaniṣadic background: ātman and brahman
The [[Upaniṣad|Upaniṣads]] taught that the innermost **self** ([[ātman]]) is unborn and undying, and that the **absolute** ([[brahman]]) underlies all things. The most radical among them taught that the two are one. The Gītā quotes them, especially the [[Kaṭha Upaniṣad]]: the self "is not born, nor does it die" {2.20}; the hierarchy of senses, mind and understanding {3.42}; the inverted world-tree {15.1}.
The [[Īśā Upaniṣad]] had already suggested that one could go on acting and live a hundred years, because "action does not cling" to the one who sees the Lord in all. The Gītā develops this into a full doctrine. It also inherits the Upaniṣadic ideal of seeing "all beings in the self and the self in all beings" {6.29}.
How the self relates to brahman, and both to the personal God who speaks in the Gītā, is the great question that divides the later Vedānta schools. The text supports more than one answer. It calls brahman "neither being nor non-being" {13.12}, yet has Kṛṣṇa declare himself "the foundation of brahman" {14.27} and the Supreme Person beyond both perishable and imperishable {15.18}.

# yogas | The three yogas and their harmony
"Yoga" in the Gītā means a disciplined way of joining oneself to the highest. Three such ways are usually distinguished. **[[karma-yoga|Karma-yoga]]**, the yoga of action, is doing one's duty without attachment to results. **[[jñāna-yoga|Jñāna-yoga]]**, the yoga of knowledge, is liberating insight into the self. **[[bhakti-yoga|Bhakti-yoga]]**, the yoga of devotion, is loving self-offering to God. A fourth, **[[dhyāna|dhyāna]]** or meditation (Chapter 6), is sometimes added.
A traditional division groups the eighteen chapters into three "hexads": 1–6 on action, 7–12 on devotion, 13–18 on knowledge. It is a useful map (see the diagram above), though each theme appears throughout.
Which yoga is highest? Here the commentators part ways. For Śaṅkara, knowledge alone liberates, and action and devotion prepare the mind for it. For Rāmānuja, devotion is the culmination, with knowledge and action as its supports. For Tilak, selfless action for the world is the Gītā's final word. Vivekananda saw the Gītā's originality precisely in *harmonizing* paths that rival schools had set against each other. A careful reader will notice that the text itself tends to say "both" where we expect "either/or": "Children, not the wise, speak of Sāṅkhya and yoga as different" {5.4}.

# god | The God of the Gītā
The speaker of the Gītā gradually reveals himself. First he is a friend and charioteer. Then he is the teacher of an ancient yoga {4.1}. Then he is the one who takes birth "age after age" to restore dharma {4.7–8}, the source and dissolution of the whole world {7.6}, and the one in whom all beings rest, though he does not rest in them {9.4–5}. Finally, in Chapter 11, he appears as the terrifying universal form, "time, the destroyer of worlds" {11.32}.
This God is both immanent and transcendent. He is "the taste in water, the light in sun and moon" {7.8}, "seated in the heart of all" {15.15}, yet "greater even than Brahmā" {11.37}. He is impartial, "no one is hateful to me, and no one is dear" {9.29}, yet loving: "you are dear to me" {18.65}. And he acts. "There is nothing I need to do, yet I continue in action" {3.22}. God himself is the model of the karma-yogin.
The Gītā's theology has been called *panentheism*: all is in God, but God is more than all. Its emphasis on grace ([[prasāda]]) and on devotion ([[bhakti]]) made it the charter text of the great devotional movements of medieval India.

# ethics | Ethical frameworks: duty, consequences, character
Readers trained in Western moral philosophy often try to place the Gītā within its categories. It invites comparison with **deontology**: do your duty because it is your duty, "because it ought to be done" {18.9}. Kant's "duty for duty's sake" is the obvious parallel. It invites comparison with **virtue ethics**: the long portraits of the sage {2.55–72}, the devotee {12.13–20}, and the divine qualities {16.1–3} describe a character rather than a rule. Its insistence that the agent give up concern for outcomes seems to set it against **consequentialism**, though Kṛṣṇa also appeals to consequences: disgrace {2.34}, and the world's cohesion ([[loka-saṅgraha]]) {3.20}.
Perhaps the Gītā's most original contribution is to the ethics of **agency and motivation**. It asks not only what to do but from where to act. An act done from fear, greed or the ego's need to win differs from the same act done from equanimity. The text even claims that one free of I-making "does not kill and is not bound, even if he kills" {18.17}. This is among the most contested claims in the text, because it seems to remove the agent from moral responsibility.
Any serious reading must also face the Gītā's **social ethics**: its acceptance of the four social orders ([[varṇa]]), its fear of their "mixing" {1.41}, and its statement that one should do one's own duty even if flawed {18.47–48}. B. R. Ambedkar read the Gītā as a defense of caste. Many modern readers reinterpret its categories as temperament and vocation. Both responses should be weighed honestly.

# commentaries | Commentators and the history of reading
Because the Gītā is one of the three foundations (*prasthāna-traya*) of Vedānta, with the Upaniṣads and the *Brahma-sūtra*, every major Vedāntic teacher wrote on it. **[[Śaṅkara]]** (8th c.) gives the oldest surviving commentary and the Advaita reading. **[[Rāmānuja]]** (11th–12th c.) gives the qualified non-dualist reading centered on devotion. **[[Madhva]]** (13th c.) gives the dualist reading. Outside Vedānta, **[[Abhinavagupta]]** (10th–11th c.) read it as a Kashmir Śaiva, and the Marathi poet **[[Jñāneśvara]]** (13th c.) turned it into a vernacular masterpiece of devotional non-dualism.
Later commentators include Madhusūdana Sarasvatī (16th c.), who tried to reconcile Advaita with fervent devotion, and Viśvanātha Cakravartin and Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa of the Bengal Vaiṣṇava tradition. That tradition's modern descendant, A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda's *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* (1968), is one of the most widely distributed books in the world.
Reading the commentaries side by side is the best training in interpretation. The same verse is made to say "renounce all action," "act without attachment," or "surrender to God," often by small differences of syntax or emphasis. The translator's notes (✦) in this companion flag such places.

# modern | The modern Gītā: nation, non-violence and the West
The Gītā's modern career is a story of its own. Charles Wilkins's English translation (1785) introduced it to Europe. Warren Hastings's preface predicted that it would survive when British rule was forgotten. Schlegel and Humboldt admired it, and Humboldt called it perhaps "the deepest and loftiest thing the world has to show." Emerson and Thoreau read it at Walden.
In India it became the scripture of the independence movement. **[[Tilak]]** read it as a call to selfless political action. **[[Aurobindo]]** read it, from prison, as a spiritual synthesis. **[[Gandhi]]**, its most famous modern devotee, called it his "spiritual dictionary" and read its war as the inner battle against selfishness, in harmony with non-violence. **[[Vivekananda]]** presented it as a philosophy of strength and of work without selfish motive.
Its influence also reached unexpected places. T. S. Eliot paraphrased it in *Four Quartets* ("And do not think of the fruit of action. Fare forward."). J. Robert Oppenheimer, who read it in Sanskrit, recalled {11.12} and {11.32} at the first atomic test. In our own century it shapes discussions of leadership, mindfulness and work, often in popular forms that trim away its theology and its violence.

# scholarship | Current scholarship and open questions
Several debates are live today. **Composition and unity**: is the Gītā a layered text or a unified composition, and how does it relate to the rest of the Mahābhārata? (Malinar, *The Bhagavadgītā: Doctrines and Contexts*, 2007; Adluri and Bagchee, *The Nay Science*, 2014.) **Religious context**: how does it respond to Buddhism and to renunciant movements, and to the ritualism of Vedic orthodoxy? **Theology**: is its final teaching non-dual, theistic, or deliberately both? (Chakravarthi Ram-Prasad, *Divine Self, Human Self*, 2013.)
**Ethics and violence**: can a text that urges a warrior to fight ground an ethic of non-violence? How should its claims about agency and responsibility be judged? (Sen, *The Idea of Justice*, 2009; Matilal, *Ethics and Epics*, 2002.) **Caste and gender**: are its social categories essential or historical? **Reception**: how did the Gītā become *the* Hindu scripture in the 19th and 20th centuries? (Richard H. Davis, *The Bhagavad Gita: A Biography*, 2015.) Psychologists have also built empirical measures of "non-attachment" (*anāsakti*) and of the guṇas, and studied their relation to wellbeing. This work is promising but early.
These debates are flagged in each chapter's "Current research" panel. They are invitations to think, not settled verdicts.

# using | Using this companion
Each chapter opens with a summary, an overview and a concept map. The chapter is then divided into sections that follow its argument (based on the traditional synopsis of the Gītā Press edition). Each section gives the verses, then its study panels.
Tap any underlined term for a definition. Select text to highlight it in one of five colors, or attach a note. Tap a verse number to highlight the whole verse or add a note to it. In diagrams, use the Highlight button to color boxes. Everything you mark is gathered in **My notes**, organized by chapter and section and searchable. **Search** looks across the verses, footnotes, analysis, glossary and your own notes at once.
A suggestion for book clubs: read one chapter aloud together, then take two or three discussion questions from the sections and one from the chapter's seminar questions.
